Let $(R,\mathfrak m)$ be a local ring of characteristic $p>0$ and dimension $d$. Its **Hilbert–Kunz function** is
$$\operatorname{HK}_e(R)=\ell\big(R/\mathfrak m^{[p^e]}\big),$$
where $\mathfrak m^{[p^e]}$ is the ideal generated by the $p^e$-th powers of the elements of $\mathfrak m$. Monsky showed [Mon83] that it always has the form
$$\operatorname{HK}_e(R)=c\,p^{de}+\Delta_e,\qquad \Delta_e=O\big(p^{(d-1)e}\big),$$
for a real number $c\geq 1$, the **Hilbert–Kunz multiplicity** $e_\text{HK}(R)$.

It would be great if $\operatorname{HK}_e(R)$ were eventually a polynomial in $p^e$, like its sister the Hilbert–Samuel function. This post is a tour of examples showing that it is not, and that the truth is much stranger. All the examples can be run below.

## Periodic correction terms

Already in dimension 1, Monsky showed in that same article that $\Delta_e$ can be periodic:

**Example (Monsky, 1983).** Let $R=\mathbb F_2[[x,y]]/(x^5+y^5)$. Then, for all $e\geq0$,
$$\operatorname{HK}_e(R)=\begin{cases}5\cdot 2^e-4 & e \text{ even},\\ 5\cdot2^e-6 & e\text{ odd}.\end{cases}$$

The function `HK_function_diagonal` computes the whole Hilbert–Kunz function with the algorithm of Han and Monsky, the subject of this series. Its result can also be evaluated, so we can compare it with a direct Gröbner basis computation:

```sage
S.<x,y> = GF(2)[]
H = HK_function_diagonal(x^5 + y^5)
print(H)
print([H(e) for e in range(7)])
print([HK_groebner(x^5 + y^5, e) for e in range(7)])
```

## Quasi-polynomials?

The next guess is that Hilbert–Kunz functions are **quasi-polynomials** in $p^e$, that is, $c_n(e)p^{en}+\dots+c_1(e)p^e+c_0(e)$ with periodic coefficients $c_i(e)$. This is also wrong, but several examples point that way:

**Kunz's example** [Kun76, Example 4.3]: $R=\mathbb F_p[[t,x_1,\dots,x_n]]/(t^n-x_1^{d_1}\cdots x_n^{d_n})$. For instance, for $t^3-x^2y^2z^2$ and $p=5$,
$$\operatorname{HK}_e(R)=\tfrac{26}{9}\,5^{3e}-\tfrac83\,5^{e}+(-1)^e\,\tfrac{16}{9}\qquad(e\geq1).$$

**Adding a variable.** An observation of Ilya Smirnov: $\operatorname{HK}_e(R[[t]])=p^e\operatorname{HK}_e(R)$. So any ring with a quasi-polynomial Hilbert–Kunz function produces higher-dimensional ones.

**Affine semigroup rings** also have quasi-polynomial Hilbert–Kunz functions.

```sage
S.<t,x,y,z> = GF(5)[]
print(HK_function_disjoint_monomials(t^3 - x^2*y^2*z^2))
```

And a quick check of Smirnov's observation, adjoining a variable to $\mathbb F_p[[x,y]]/(x^3+y^4)$:

```sage
for p, e in [(3, 1), (3, 2), (5, 1), (5, 2)]:
    A.<x,y> = GF(p)[]; B.<x,y,t> = GF(p)[]
    hkA = A.ideal([x^3 + y^4] + [g^(p^e) for g in A.gens()]).vector_space_dimension()
    hkB = B.ideal([x^3 + y^4] + [g^(p^e) for g in B.gens()]).vector_space_dimension()
    print(p, e, hkA, hkB, hkB == p^e * hkA)
```

## Han and Monsky's surprising example

In 1993, Han and Monsky [HM93] gave a method to compute the Hilbert–Kunz function of every **diagonal hypersurface** $x_1^{d_1}+\dots+x_n^{d_n}$, with examples like this one:

**Example (Han–Monsky, 1993).** For all $e\geq0$,
$$\operatorname{HK}_e\left(\frac{\mathbb F_5[[x,y,z,t]]}{(x^4+y^4+z^4+t^4)}\right)=\frac{168}{61}\,5^{3e}-\frac{107}{61}\,3^e.$$

```sage
S.<x,y,z,t> = GF(5)[]
HK_function_diagonal(x^4 + y^4 + z^4 + t^4)
```

This might suggest that Hilbert–Kunz functions are quasi-polynomials *in several variables*, here $5^e$ and $3^e$. In fact, Han and Monsky showed:

**Theorem ([HM93]).** Let $p$ be a prime and $d_1,\dots,d_n\geq2$. Then there are integers $e_0\geq0$, $\mu\geq1$ and $\ell\geq0$ such that
$$\operatorname{HK}_e\left(\frac{\mathbb F_p[[x_1,\dots,x_n]]}{(x_1^{d_1}+\dots+x_n^{d_n})}\right)=c\,p^{(n-1)e}+c_1(e)\,\ell^{\lfloor e/\mu\rfloor}\qquad\text{for all } e\geq e_0,$$
where $c\geq1$ is rational and $e\mapsto c_1(e)$ is periodic of period $\mu$. Moreover, if $n\geq3$ then $0\leq\ell\leq p^{(n-3)\mu}$, and if $p=2$ or $n\in\{2,3\}$ then $\ell\in\{0,1\}$.

Writing $e=\mu\lfloor e/\mu\rfloor+k(e)$ with $0\leq k(e)<\mu$, we get $\ell^{\lfloor e/\mu\rfloor}=\ell^{-k(e)/\mu}\,(\ell^{1/\mu})^e$, a quasi-polynomial in the variable $\ell^{1/\mu}$. It is up to the reader to judge whether we want to allow irrational variables, and hence be happy throwing diagonal hypersurfaces into the "quasi-polynomial in several variables" drawer. For example, here $\mu=2$ and $\ell=3$:

```sage
S.<x,y,z,t> = GF(3)[]
HK_function_diagonal(x^6 + y^6 + z^6 + t^5)
```

## Beyond diagonal hypersurfaces

A few years later, Chiang and Hung [CH98] extended these techniques to hypersurfaces of the form $\sum_i\prod_j x_{i,j}^{d_{i,j}}$, where different monomials use different variables. Their examples show that Hilbert–Kunz functions need not even be combinations of periodic functions and powers:

**Example ([CH98]).** For every $e\geq0$,
$$\operatorname{HK}_e\left(\frac{\mathbb F_7[[x,y,z,s,t,u,v]]}{((xyzs)^6+t^6+u^6+v^6)}\right)=\frac{2050747718345259}{429658164277013}\,7^{6e}-\frac{267597}{74480}\,7^{3e}+\left(\frac{164565}{2801}\,e-\frac{312186671451}{878707312}\right)7^{e}+\frac{104440051}{294115}\,3^{e}.$$

The coefficient of $7^e$ is **linear in $e$**.

```sage
S.<x,y,z,s,t,u,v> = GF(7)[]
HK_function_disjoint_monomials((x*y*z*s)^6 + t^6 + u^6 + v^6)
```

All these examples have rational **Hilbert–Kunz series** $\sum_e\operatorname{HK}_e(R)z^e$, which forces the Hilbert–Kunz multiplicity to be rational. But irrational Hilbert–Kunz multiplicities exist [Bre13], and Monsky gave an explicit conjectural example, $x^3+y^3+xyz+uv$ over $\mathbb F_2$ [Mon08], whose Hilbert–Kunz series involves $(1-2z)^{3/2}$.

## What comes next

The goal of this series is to explain the tools behind Han and Monsky's theorem, and to use them to actually compute the Hilbert–Kunz function of any diagonal hypersurface. Even decades later, this machinery keeps giving insight into Hilbert–Kunz functions of hypersurfaces [CH98, MT04, MT06, Cas25, Men26b, PSSY26].

The whole computation comes down to understanding the function
$$D_k\left(\frac{a_1}{d_1},\dots,\frac{a_n}{d_n}\right)=\frac{1}{d_1\cdots d_n}\dim_k\frac{k[x_1,\dots,x_n]}{(x_1^{a_1},\dots,x_n^{a_n},x_1^{d_1}+\dots+x_n^{d_n})}.$$
Han's thesis [Han92] proved a functional equation for $D_k$, provided that a certain function $\ell_k$ is *$p$-multiplicative*. Han proved this for $p>2$ and $n=2,3$, and for $p=2$. Monsky later developed the theory of the **Han–Monsky ring**, which reinterprets $D_k$ and $\ell_k$ and proves the functional equation in full generality. That ring is the subject of the next post.
